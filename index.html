Part 01

Then

How engineers used to build software

Let's start with a short trip back in time, so you can see how much the job has changed.
30 years in one line

Four eras of building software

In about 30 years, engineering went through four big eras. Each one let a single engineer build much more, and each one changed what it took to be good at the job. Watch the dashed line: what one person can build keeps climbing, and right now it's climbing faster than ever.
1990s
Era 01 · C

Making it compile was the mission

Memory by handHours of focusOne-language experts
inventory.c
/* allocate, copy, pray */
char *buf = malloc(256);
strcpy(buf, input);     /* hope it fits */
free(buf);
free(buf);              /* oops */

$ gcc inventory.c -o inventory
inventory.c:57: error: expected ';' before '}'
$ gcc inventory.c -o inventory && ./inventory
Segmentation fault (core dumped)
$ █
In the C days you managed memory by hand. Forget to free it and you leak; free it twice and the program crashes with "segmentation fault" and no hint where. Just making code compile and run was the mission. It took deep knowledge, hours of concentration, and calculating memory sizes and pointers in your head. People spent years mastering one language and stayed experts in it.
2000s
Era 02 · The web

Every layer, and how they talk

Full stackHTTPSQL
Then came the web. A single feature now crossed three tiers: the browser, the application server, and the database. You had to understand each layer and how they talk to each other: HTTP between browser and server, SQL between server and database. "Full-stack" became a job title, but most engineers still picked a side: frontend, backend, or data.
2010s
Era 03 · Cloud & big data

Hundreds of services. Choose, wire, scale.

Distributed systemsDevOpsPlatform experts
Cloud and big data turned the toolbox into hundreds of managed services: compute, storage, queues, streaming, warehouses, containers. The hard part moved from writing every line to choosing the right pieces and wiring them together so they scale. Engineers became platform specialists: the AWS person, the Kafka person, the Spark person.

The skills behind each era

Less memory. More judgment.

Bigger dot = mattered more.

Look at how the skills shifted. Memorizing syntax and mastering one language mattered most in the C era and matter least today. What grew: knowing many technologies, seeing the whole system, learning new tools fast, and judgment, meaning you can look at a piece of work and say whether it's good. Here's the job rebalanced. Typing goes down: you write far fewer lines by hand. Velocity goes way up: going from an idea to a working prototype can take hours instead of weeks. Breadth goes up: one engineer can now work across frontend, backend, data and cloud, because the AI fills in the syntax gaps. And judgment goes up the most, because someone has to decide what's actually good enough to ship.

Part 03

Judgment

What AI doesn't do for you

So if the AI writes the code, what's left for the engineer? A lot. This is the part that makes or breaks you.
What AI doesn't do for you

It writes the tip. You own the iceberg.

This is the key slide. AI writes the part you can see: the code. Everything below the water is still on you. Is the architecture right? What are the alternatives? Is it tested? Will we know if it breaks in production? Is it secure? Can the next person change it safely? That's the difference between an engineer and someone who just types prompts.
See the big picture

The AI will pick one. You need to know why.

CloudAWSvsGCPvsAzure
DatabasePostgresvsMongoDBvsBigQuery
DesignMonolithvsMicroservices
Data flowBatchvsStreaming
AI toolsClaude CodevsCursorvsCopilot
The AI will happily make choices for you: a database, a cloud service, a design. You need to know the options and why you'd pick one over another: AWS or GCP, Postgres or MongoDB or BigQuery, a monolith or microservices, batch or streaming. The same applies to the AI tools themselves; Claude Code, Cursor and Copilot each fit different situations. Knowing the alternatives is what lets you say "no, not like that."

This is a discipline, and it's very learnable.
Part 04

Risks

Where AI coding goes wrong

AI coding is powerful, but it comes with real risks. Here are the two I see most often.
Risk 01

More code is not better code

Hard to maintainHidden bugs
The first big risk: AI makes it easy to produce huge amounts of code. The amount of code grows much faster than the amount anyone actually understands. That gap is where bugs hide and where systems become painful to maintain. Every line you ship is a line someone has to own.
Risk 02

It does what you said, not what you meant

Illustrative example
You"Clean up the inactive users."
AIDELETE FROM users WHERE last_login < NOW() - INTERVAL '30 days';
✓ done, confidently
MeantFree accounts, inactive 2+ years. Archive them, don't delete.
ResultThousands of paying customers gone on Monday morning.
The second risk: the AI does exactly what you said, not what you meant. In this made-up but realistic example, someone asked to clean up inactive users. The AI picked 30 days on its own and deleted them. The product team meant free accounts inactive for two years, and wanted them archived, not deleted. The AI rarely says "I'm not sure." Clear briefs and careful review are your defense.

Before questions, three tips for engineering interviews today. First, build a real application you're passionate about. Make it work, put the code in a public GitHub repo, and talk about it. A candidate who built something real is always impressive. Second, know why you want to be an engineer. Maybe you love building things, maybe you love working with customers, maybe you love complex puzzles. Love for the job goes a long way. Third, in technical questions we want to see how you think.
